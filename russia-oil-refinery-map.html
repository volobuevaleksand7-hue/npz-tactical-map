Where Are Russia's Oil Refineries Located?

Russia runs roughly thirty large-scale crude processing plants. They fall into five geographic clusters: the Volga-Urals belt, Western Siberia, the Northwest around Leningrad Region, the central corridor near Moscow and Ryazan, and the southern facilities along the Black Sea and Caspian coast. Together they give the country the world's third-largest refining capacity — behind only the United States and China — with total installed throughput exceeding five million barrels per day.

Historically, Volga-Urals has been the backbone of domestic fuel output. Samara, Ufa, Nizhnekamsk — these cities host some of the oldest plants, now heavily modernized, built close to legacy onshore fields and tied into a dense pipeline network reaching both domestic markets and export terminals. The region alone covers a substantial share of national diesel and gasoline output.

Western Siberia centers on Omsk, close to the vast producing fields of Khanty-Mansi and Yamal-Nenets — a natural processing hub, sparing crude the long haul west. The Omsk facility often ranks among the largest single-site refineries on the continent.

The Northwest cluster is anchored by the Kirishi plant in Leningrad Region. Its main job is feeding export flows through Baltic ports like Primorsk and Ust-Luga — which makes it strategically important for shipments to European buyers, and increasingly, via maritime routes, to Asian ones too.

Central Russia's sites, including Moscow Refinery and the Ryazan plant, exist to supply the capital and its surrounding industrial zones. Here proximity to the country's largest fuel-consumption center matters more than export logistics.

Finally comes the southern grouping near Volgograd, Tuapse, and the Krasnodar area, which ties refining output to Black Sea export infrastructure — covering both regional demand and seaborne trade toward the Mediterranean.

Why does this five-cluster map matter? Each region faces its own logistics, feedstock access, and export exposure — factors that shape vulnerability to sanctions, infrastructure strikes, or pipeline disruptions, and explain why trouble in one zone rarely spreads evenly across the whole national system.

Which Are the Largest Oil Refineries in Russia by Capacity?

The single largest oil refinery in Russia by throughput capacity is the Omsk Refinery, owned by Gazprom Neft, processing roughly 440,000 barrels per day (around 22.23 million tonnes annually).

A couple of patterns stand out here. Ownership is concentrated among a handful of vertically integrated groups — Rosneft, Lukoil, Gazprom Neft, Surgutneftegas, and Tatneft — each running multiple processing sites rather than betting everything on one flagship plant. Geography matters too. The heaviest-capacity facilities cluster either near major crude-producing basins in Siberia (Omsk, Angarsk) or close to export infrastructure and population centers in the European part of the country (Kirishi, Ryazan, Volgograd, Yaroslavl) — a layout that keeps transport costs down, whether for feedstock or finished fuel.

Published capacity figures vary depending on the metric used, and that's worth keeping in mind. Nameplate capacity — what a plant is engineered to handle — often differs from actual annual throughput, which shifts with maintenance schedules, crude availability, and domestic demand for specific fuel grades. Even the units matter: barrels per day versus million tonnes per year can produce slightly different rankings, since the conversion factor depends on the density of each crude blend, and density varies by source field. So treat the table above as an approximate ordering of scale, not an audited ranking — it's a guide to relative importance within the national refining base, not a tool for exact capacity comparisons.

Which Companies Dominate Russia's Refining Capacity?

Rosneft controls the single largest share of the country's oil-processing industry. As of 2023, it operates roughly 40% of national refining capacity — a position no other company comes close to matching. That dominance rests on a portfolio of large-scale plants acquired through decades of consolidation, including facilities inherited from the Yukos breakup and the integration of Bashneft's downstream assets. The scale alone gives the company outsized influence over domestic fuel supply, pricing dynamics, and the pace of modernization projects across the sector.

Behind Rosneft sits a handful of vertically integrated groups, and together they account for most of the remaining processing output. Gazprom Neft runs the Omsk refinery, one of the technologically most advanced plants in the country and a long-standing benchmark for deep conversion rates and light-product yield. Surgutneftegaz, meanwhile, operates Kirishinefteorgsintez — a major northwestern facility that has historically supplied export-oriented fuel oil and diesel to European-adjacent markets. Its product mix has since shifted, though, following sanctions-driven trade reorientation toward Asia.

Lukoil holds a meaningful share of total throughput through refineries in Perm, Nizhny Novgorod, and Volgograd. Its overseas assets, by contrast, have faced growing scrutiny amid shifting geopolitical alignments. Tatneft is smaller in absolute volume but strategically important regionally: it runs the Taneco complex in Tatarstan, built relatively recently and designed with higher-complexity units than much of the Soviet-era legacy fleet. Smaller independent and regional operators fill out the remainder, though their combined footprint stays modest next to the five majors.

So much volume concentrated in so few hands has a direct consequence: disruptions at any one flagship plant — whether from drone strikes, unplanned outages, or sanctions on specific terminals — can move national fuel-market indicators almost immediately. It also means government energy policy, from export-duty formulas to modernization subsidies, gets negotiated with a small circle of players rather than a fragmented market. Knowing which company controls which plant, then, is essential context for interpreting almost any news about fuel shortages, price spikes, or planned infrastructure upgrades in the sector.

How Did 2026 Drone Strikes Affect Russian Refining Output?

In July 2026, average daily crude processing in Russia fell to roughly 3.6 million barrels — the lowest monthly figure since May 2002. A sustained wave of Ukrainian unmanned-aerial attacks had forced repeated shutdowns at plants across the country. Two different numbers circulated at the time, and it's worth keeping them apart. An operational snapshot taken in the first days of July showed throughput briefly touching something closer to 3.91 million barrels per day — at the time cited as the lowest since March 2005. That early reading was later superseded by consolidated data covering the full month, which settled near the lower 3.6 million mark (lowest since May 2002) once cumulative outages were fully accounted for. The early-month figure captured a moment before several facilities went offline again; the monthly average captured something deeper — the full extent of the damage.

This wasn't a single-event shock. It was the cumulative result of a months-long campaign. Plants that had already absorbed strikes earlier in the year — including large Rosneft and Lukoil-linked facilities such as the Ryazan, Volgograd, Novokuibyshevsk, Afipsky and Tuapse refineries — got hit again, or kept running at reduced rates while repair crews worked through fire damage, destroyed distillation units and disrupted pipelines. Each new wave of drone sorties knocked out a portion of capacity for days or weeks. And because several of these sites had already been targeted more than once since 2024, their recovery kept stretching out. Losses compounded rather than cleared between incidents.

The pattern through spring and early summer of 2026 was one of intermittent dips followed by partial recoveries. July broke that rhythm: losses outpaced repairs across multiple regions at once. The effect went beyond a short-term disruption — it became a structural drag on the country's processing base, pushing some operators to redirect crude toward export rather than domestic refining and tightening the supply of refined products in affected regions. Analysts tracking the sector saw in the two-decade-low reading a sign of just how hard it had become to keep pace with the frequency and precision of the strikes hitting Russia's downstream energy infrastructure that summer.

How Much Refining Capacity Is Offline in Russia Right Now?

No single confirmed figure captures how much Russian refining capacity sits idle in October 2026. The two numbers people cite most often don't even describe the same thing: one is a point-in-time outage tally from September 2024, the other a forward-looking throughput forecast from the International Energy Agency (IEA) covering the rest of 2026 and into 2027.

The gap between these rows matters. The 2024 Reuters number is a historical snapshot — plants that had actually stopped running, mostly because of drone-strike damage and the repair time that followed. The IEA figure works differently: it's a forecast of how much lower average processing output could run over an 18-month-plus stretch, shaped by sanctions pressure, equipment and parts shortages, and continued attrition from attacks on infrastructure. A ~30% drop in throughput isn't the same claim as "30% of nameplate capacity sitting dark." Some of that decline could simply come from refineries running at reduced rates rather than shutting down entirely.

Refinery damage and repair cycles shift month to month, so the real current state could sit anywhere between the 2024 snapshot and the IEA's worse-case trajectory. One thing the comparison does show clearly: direction. Outages that were already meaningful two years ago are, by the IEA's own modeling, expected to deepen rather than resolve over the medium term — with direct implications for both domestic fuel supply and the export volumes available to international buyers.

How Can You Monitor Russian Refinery Status in Real Time?

  1. Скомбинируйте минимум четыре типа источников и сверяйте их между собой. Это и есть прямой ответ: надёжное отслеживание состояния нефтеперерабатывающих заводов в реальном времени не держится на одном канале — оно строится на пересечении спутниковых снимков, карт ударов, сводок энергетических агентств и данных по грузоперевозкам. Один источник почти всегда даёт неполную или задержанную картину.

  2. Начните со спутниковых платформ открытого доступа: Sentinel Hub на базе снимков Copernicus, Planet Labs, NASA FIRMS для тепловых аномалий. Найдите координаты нужного завода и сравните снимки «до» и «после» предполагаемого инцидента. Задымление, обугленные резервуары, повреждённые трубопроводы — всё это видно даже на изображениях среднего разрешения.

  3. Тепловые точки (thermal hotspots) на FIRMS или похожих сервисах стоит проверить отдельно. Активный пожар или работающая установка дают характерную сигнатуру, обновляется она несколько раз в сутки — и это самый быстрый способ зафиксировать момент удара.

  4. Снимок стоит сверить с геолокационными картами ударов — их ведут независимые OSINT-аккаунты и мониторинговые проекты в соцсетях. Координаты и видео там обычно появляются в течение нескольких часов после события. Подлинность каждого эпизода всё же лучше перепроверять по нескольким источникам.

  5. Дальше — ожидание подтверждений, официальных или полуофициальных. Региональные власти, пресс-службы компаний, местные СМИ нередко сообщают о приостановке работы завода или снижении мощности, и именно это помогает отличить разовое повреждение от длительного простоя.

  6. Пригодятся и отчёты профильных энергетических агентств и аналитических центров — регулярные обзоры по переработке, экспорту, ценам на топливо. В них виден совокупный эффект на объёмы производства, иногда упоминается и статус конкретных предприятий.

  7. Есть и косвенные индикаторы: данные судового трекинга (AIS) по танкерам, заходящим в ближайшие терминалы, биржевые котировки на нефтепродукты. Резкие изменения в этих метриках часто сигнализируют о перебоях раньше, чем появляются официальные сообщения.

  8. Каждое наблюдение стоит фиксировать — с датой, координатами, ссылкой на источник, в отдельной таблице. Так со временем складывается собственная хронология, которую легко перепроверять и обновлять по мере поступления новых снимков.

Why Does Russia's Refinery Map Matter for Global Oil Markets?

Russia's network of oil-processing plants shapes global fuel prices. The country remains one of the largest exporters of diesel and other refined products, so any disruption to its processing capacity ripples far beyond its own borders. A facility goes offline — from a drone strike, a fire, scheduled maintenance, or sanctions-related equipment shortages — and the immediate effect is a drop in domestic output of diesel, naphtha, and jet fuel. The secondary effect, though, lands on international markets within days or weeks: traders reprice contracts, buyers scramble for alternative supply.

Geography matters so much here because these plants aren't evenly distributed or interchangeable. Facilities near the Black Sea and Baltic coasts feed export terminals directly — an outage there hits international shipments almost immediately. Inland complexes work differently: they mostly serve domestic consumption and regional pipelines. So a strike on a coastal, export-oriented site carries a very different market signature than one on a plant hundreds of kilometers from the nearest port. The former tightens seaborne diesel supply to Europe, Africa, and Asia; the latter mostly raises local fuel prices, with little international echo.

Capacity matters too, independent of location. Russia built some of its largest conversion units specifically to maximize diesel yield for export — European and African markets historically paid a premium for this fuel over gasoline. Damage one of those units, and the loss isn't just barrels of crude throughput. It's a disproportionate loss of the exact product grade that global shipping, agriculture, and trucking fleets depend on, because diesel demand is far less elastic than gasoline demand in the short term.

That's why analysts, commodity traders, even logistics companies abroad track outages at individual Russian plants almost in real time. One large facility losing a third of its distillation capacity can move Rotterdam or Singapore diesel benchmarks within a single trading session — long before the barrel count ever shows up in an official export statistic.
